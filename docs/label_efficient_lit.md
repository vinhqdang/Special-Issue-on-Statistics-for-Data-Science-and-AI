# Literature sweep: label-efficient comparison of several classifiers on group-fairness metrics

Date of sweep: 4 November 2026.

Target idea under test: for K trained classifiers, a large unlabeled pool with known group membership, and a fixed labelling budget, compare the models on group-specific TPR, FPR, error and the equalised-odds gap. We stratify the pool by (group x disagreement pattern of the K models), allocate labels by a Neyman-type rule that uses the models' predicted probabilities as proxies for the unknown labels, estimate with Horvitz-Thompson / ratio estimators, and build confidence intervals by linearisation. The motivating facts: (1) differences in error, TPR or FPR between two models come only from instances where they disagree (3-11% of instances for competing good models), and (2) group membership is known before labelling, so small groups can be oversampled.

## How this was done and what the limits are

- Sources queried: the arXiv API (about 60 title/abstract queries), the Crossref API (about 40 bibliographic queries and DOI look-ups), web search (about 12 queries), and publisher or proceedings pages (PMLR, NeurIPS proceedings metadata, OUP, Springer listing, Scientific Reports via Crossref).
- Every reference below was checked against at least one of: Crossref DOI record, arXiv record, PMLR/NeurIPS/ACL/OUP page. Venue is given only when one of these sources shows it; otherwise the paper is listed as an arXiv preprint.
- "Basis" says how much of each paper was seen: full text (downloaded and searched), abstract, or record only (title, authors, venue). A summary built on a record only is marked as such.
- Not covered: Google Scholar, Scopus and Web of Science were not reachable, and dblp blocked API access from this environment. Some Springer and Nature pages sat behind redirects, so for some items only the Crossref record or abstract was read. The sweep is systematic but not exhaustive. The diagnostic-test literature (two-phase designs, verification bias) is large and only its main entry points were checked. Re-run before submission.

## 1. Closest prior work (read these first)

### 1.1 Sawade, Landwehr and Scheffer (2012), Active comparison of prediction models

- Reference: Sawade, C., Landwehr, N. and Scheffer, T. (2012). Active Comparison of Prediction Models. Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 25, pp. 1754-1762.
- Link: https://papers.nips.cc/paper/4495-active-comparison-of-prediction-models (metadata: https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/file/92fb0c6d1758261f10d052e6e2c1123c-Metadata.json)
- Basis: full text (proceedings metadata file), searched for the key passages.
- Summary: compares the risks of two fixed models on a labelling budget using an importance-sampling (instrumental) distribution chosen to maximise the power of a test of the risk difference. Theorem 1 gives the optimal distribution q*(x) proportional to p(x) times the square root of E[(loss_1 - loss_2 - Delta)^2 | x], and pool-based plug-in versions use the models' own predictive distributions as proxies for p(y|x). The paper says outright that the distribution "gives preference to test instances on which the models disagree strongly". It also includes a baseline that samples only where f1(x) != f2(x) and notes that this coincides with the A2 and IWAL active-learning algorithms. For more than two models it uses a heuristic mixture of the pairwise-optimal distributions.
- Closeness: high for ideas (1) and the proxy-based optimal allocation. It already uses disagreement to compare error rates, applies to several models through the mixture, and plugs in model probabilities as proxies.
- Differences: error/risk only (zero-one or squared loss), with no groups, TPR/FPR or fairness. Sampling is importance sampling with replacement, not stratification by group x disagreement pattern. There is no ratio estimator for conditional rates, and no simultaneous inference over pairs and metrics.

### 1.2 Comparing two diagnostic or screening tests by verifying a subset (biostatistics)

This literature already exploits the fact that paired comparisons of TPR and FPR depend on discordant results, and that only relative accuracy is identified when concordant cells are not verified.

- Pepe, M. S. and Alonzo, T. A. (2001). Comparing disease screening tests when true disease status is ascertained only for screen positives. Biostatistics 2(3):249-260. DOI 10.1093/biostatistics/2.3.249. (Crossref lists only the first author; the OUP page lists both.) Basis: abstract on the OUP page. With verification only for people positive on at least one test, absolute TPR/FPR are not identified, but the ratio of TPRs and the ratio of FPRs are, by analogy with relative risk in case-control studies. A marginal regression framework models covariate effects on relative accuracy.
- Schatzkin, A., Connor, R. J., Taylor, P. R. and Bunnag, B. (1987). Comparing new and old screening tests when a reference procedure cannot be performed on all screenees. American Journal of Epidemiology 125(4):672-678. DOI 10.1093/oxfordjournals.aje.a114580. Basis: Crossref record plus search-engine descriptions (abstract not read directly). The reported design applies the reference procedure only to people positive on the old or the new test, and compares the tests through ratios of sensitivities and specificities.
- Chock, C., Irwig, L., Berry, G. and Glasziou, P. (1997). Comparing dichotomous screening tests when individuals negative on both tests are not verified. Journal of Clinical Epidemiology 50:1211-1217. DOI 10.1016/s0895-4356(97)00122-4. Basis: Crossref record only (the title states the design).
- McNamee, R. (2002). Optimal designs of two-stage studies for estimation of sensitivity, specificity and positive predictive value. Statistics in Medicine 21:3609-3625. DOI 10.1002/sim.1318. Basis: abstract (Crossref). A cheap first-stage test splits subjects into categories, an expensive gold standard is applied at stage two, and optimal second-stage allocation is derived for a given cost ratio and compared with proportional allocation. This is the two-phase, stratified, optimally allocated design for sensitivity and specificity of one test.
- Begg, C. B. and Greenes, R. A. (1983). Assessment of diagnostic tests when disease verification is subject to selection bias. Biometrics 39:207 (first page per Crossref). DOI 10.2307/2530820. Basis: record only. The standard reference for correcting verification bias when verification depends on test results.
- Hadgu, A. (1996). The discrepancy in discrepant analysis. The Lancet 348:592-593. DOI 10.1016/s0140-6736(96)05122-7. Basis: record only. The title marks it as a critique of "discrepant analysis", in which only discordant results are resolved with a reference standard. Cite it as a warning against assuming that concordant results are correct.
- Closeness: high for idea (1) applied to TPR and FPR, and for the two-phase stratified design with optimal allocation (McNamee). Our TPR/FPR contrasts between models are this design applied to classifiers.
- Differences: two tests, not K models. There are no protected groups and no fairness gaps. The strata are the test results themselves, not continuous scores used as proxies. Several designs leave the double-negative cell unverified on purpose, so they identify only relative measures. Our design samples every cell with known positive probability, so the differences themselves (not only ratios) are identified, at the price of spending some labels in the agreement cells.

### 1.3 Musgrove, Radtke and Haddad (2023), discordant pair analysis

- Reference: Musgrove, D., Radtke, A. and Haddad, T. (2023). Discordant pair analysis for sample efficient model evaluation. Scientific Reports 13:20987. DOI 10.1038/s41598-023-48017-4.
- Basis: abstract (Crossref).
- Summary: evaluates a new classification algorithm against a known-performance baseline algorithm on a large unlabeled set by adjudicating only the examples the two algorithms classify differently. Overall performance is recovered by combining these adjudications with an assumed class distribution. In simulations, adjudications fall by over 90% with sensitivity and specificity preserved.
- Closeness: high for idea (1) (labels only where models disagree, giving sensitivity and specificity).
- Differences: two algorithms, one of them with known performance; it relies on an assumed class distribution and on the baseline's known accuracy instead of sampling agreement cells; no groups or fairness; no stratified design or design-based confidence intervals as far as the abstract shows.

### 1.4 Kuboyama (2026), label certificates for model choice

- Reference: Kuboyama, T. (2026). How Many Labels Does Model Choice Need? Certificates and Budgets for Selective Prediction. arXiv:2609.18622 (preprint).
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18622
- Basis: abstract and searched full text.
- Summary: studies how many labels are needed to fix the winner among K candidates, for accuracy and for the area under the generalised risk-coverage curve. Section 6.2 shows that labelling every row where at least two candidates disagree "settles the accuracy choice in all 108 conditions" (disagreement rows were 13.5-36.5% of the pool), but never settles the selective-prediction metric.
- Closeness: medium. It confirms idea (1) empirically for K models and accuracy.
- Differences: exact certification on a finite pool (labels everything in the disagreement set), not sampling-based estimation; no groups or fairness; no confidence intervals for population contrasts.

### 1.5 Kher, Do, Zhong and Smyth (2026), selecting fair classifiers online

- Reference: Kher, S., Do, H., Zhong, J. and Smyth, P. (2026). Bayesian Learning to Select Fair Classifiers. arXiv:2610.20514 (preprint, 14 October 2026).
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20514
- Basis: abstract.
- Summary: online selection, from a stream, of the agent (classifier, LLM or human labeller) that best balances predictive performance and subgroup fairness, with delayed labels. Formulated as a Bayesian multi-armed bandit with Thompson sampling, with guarantees on the selected agent covering both accuracy and fairness.
- Closeness: medium-high in goal. It is the only work found that selects among several models on accuracy and subgroup fairness with label costs.
- Differences: online bandit with regret as the target, where the policy decides which agent to query; not pool-based design of which instances to label; no use of disagreement or stratification; Bayesian, not design-based estimation with frequentist intervals for contrasts.

## 2. Active testing and active evaluation of a single model

- Kossen, J., Farquhar, S., Gal, Y. and Rainforth, T. (2021). Active Testing: Sample-Efficient Model Evaluation. Proceedings of the 38th ICML, PMLR 139:5753-5763. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/kossen21a.html; arXiv:2103.05331. Basis: abstract. Selects test points to label using acquisition functions tailored to evaluation and removes the resulting bias with an importance-weighted estimator (LURE) that also reduces variance. Closeness: medium (same "label few test points" goal). Differences: one model, overall risk, adaptive sequential sampling without replacement, no groups or comparison.
- Kossen, J., Farquhar, S., Gal, Y. and Rainforth, T. (2022). Active Surrogate Estimators: An Active Learning Approach to Label-Efficient Model Evaluation. NeurIPS 35, pp. 24557-24570. DOI 10.52202/068431-1783; arXiv:2202.06881. Basis: abstract. Estimates risk by interpolating errors with an actively learned surrogate rather than a Monte Carlo estimator. Closeness: low-medium (model-based rather than design-based). One model, no fairness.
- Berrada, G., Kossen, J., Bickford Smith, F., Razzak, M., Gal, Y. and Rainforth, T. (2025). Scaling Up Active Testing to Large Language Models. NeurIPS 38, pp. 192365-192392. DOI 10.52202/085713-5777; arXiv:2508.09093. Basis: abstract. Cheap in-context surrogates for acquisition plus a bootstrap estimate of evaluation error. Closeness: low. One model.
- Ashouritaklimi, K., Kilian, V., Huang, D., Rainforth, T. and Caron, F. (2026). Prediction-Powered Active Testing. arXiv:2607.08347 (preprint). Basis: abstract. Combines LURE with a prediction-powered control variate, derives variance-minimising acquisition rules, and proves asymptotic normality, giving valid confidence intervals. Closeness: medium in machinery (proxy predictions plus a design-unbiased estimator, CLT intervals). Differences: one model, overall risk, no groups or between-model contrasts.
- Liu, Z., Zhang, J., Liu, C. and Zhu, Y. (2026). Active Testing of Large Language Models via Approximate Neyman Allocation. arXiv:2605.10075 (preprint). Basis: abstract. Stratifies the evaluation pool by surrogate semantic entropy and uses approximate Neyman allocation from surrogate signals, coming close to an oracle Neyman allocation. Closeness: medium-high for the design (proxy-based strata plus Neyman allocation). Differences: one model, mean score on generative tasks, no groups, no comparison of models.
- Purificato, A., Bucarelli, M. S., Bacciu, A., Silvestri, F. and Mantrach, A. (2026). Select, Label, Evaluate: Active Testing in NLP. Proceedings of EMNLP 2026, pp. 26566-26584. DOI 10.18653/2026.emnlp-main.1160 (as given on arXiv:2603.21840). Basis: abstract. Benchmark of active-testing methods over 18 NLP datasets, plus an adaptive stopping rule. Closeness: low. Useful as a source of baselines.
- Sawade, C., Landwehr, N. and Scheffer, T. (2010). Active Estimation of F-Measures. NeurIPS 23, pp. 2083-2091. https://papers.nips.cc/paper/3999-active-estimation-of-f-measures. Basis: abstract (proceedings metadata). Variance-optimal instrumental distribution for estimating an F-measure (a ratio of expectations). Closeness: medium for estimating a ratio-type metric under a non-uniform design. One model, no groups.
- Ji, D., Logan, R. L., Smyth, P. and Steyvers, M. (2021). Active Bayesian Assessment of Black-Box Classifiers. Proceedings of AAAI 35(9). DOI 10.1609/aaai.v35i9.16968; arXiv:2002.06532. Basis: abstract. Bayesian uncertainty for accuracy, cost and calibration error, with Thompson-sampling-type selection of instances to label. Closeness: low-medium. Bayesian, one model at a time.
- Poms, F. et al. (2021). Low-Shot Validation: Active Importance Sampling for Estimating Classifier Performance on Rare Categories. ICCV 2021. DOI 10.1109/iccv48922.2021.01053; arXiv:2109.05720. Basis: abstract. Calibration plus importance sampling for F-scores of rare classes, with a single-trial variance estimator. Closeness: low-medium (rare cells, comparable to small groups). One model.
- Yilmaz, E., Hayes, P., Habib, R., Burgess, J. and Barber, D. (2021). Sample Efficient Model Evaluation. arXiv:2109.12043 (preprint). Basis: abstract. Minimal-error importance-sampling and Poisson-sampling designs for accuracy and F1, with confidence intervals; Poisson sampling does better. Closeness: medium for the design-based estimator and intervals. One model.
- Marchant, N. G. and Rubinstein, B. I. P. (2017). In Search of an Entity Resolution OASIS: Optimal Asymptotic Sequential Importance Sampling. Proceedings of the VLDB Endowment (2017). DOI 10.14778/3137628.3137642; arXiv:1703.00617. Basis: abstract. Adaptive importance sampling with asymptotically optimal variance for F-measure, precision and recall under extreme class imbalance. Closeness: low-medium. One classifier.
- Bennett, P. N. and Carvalho, V. R. (2010). Online stratified sampling: evaluating classifiers at web-scale. Proceedings of CIKM 2010, pp. 1581-1584. DOI 10.1145/1871437.1871677. Basis: Crossref record plus search-engine excerpts of the abstract. Stratified sampling to estimate the precision of deployed classifiers, with an online algorithm approximating the optimal disproportionate allocation; reported about 20% label savings. Closeness: medium for score-based strata with optimal allocation. One classifier, precision only.
- Kumar, A. and Raj, B. (2018). Classifier Risk Estimation Under Limited Labeling Resources. PAKDD 2018, LNCS, pp. 3-15. DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-93034-3_1; arXiv:1607.02665. Basis: abstract. Stratified-sampling strategies (strata from classifier output) for estimating accuracy, with variance reductions over 65% against simple random sampling. Closeness: medium (the closest classic "classifier evaluation with stratified sampling"). One classifier.
- Katariya, N., Iyer, A. and Sarawagi, S. (2012). Active Evaluation of Classifiers on Large Datasets. IEEE ICDM 2012, pp. 329-338. DOI 10.1109/icdm.2012.161. Basis: Crossref record plus search-engine description. Adaptive refinement of a stratified sampling scheme for accuracy estimation, with strata from learned hash functions. Closeness: medium-low. One classifier.
- Marsigli, L. and Lopez de Haro, A. (2026). Label-Efficient Monitoring of Classification Models via Stratified Importance Sampling. arXiv:2601.22326 (preprint). Basis: abstract. Stratified importance sampling with one-shot batch label acquisition; unbiased, with finite-sample MSE improvements over both IS and stratified random sampling, even with noisy proxies. Closeness: medium for the design (one-shot batch, proxy-driven strata). One model, no fairness.

## 3. Active model selection and label-efficient model comparison

- Karimi, M. R., Gurel, N. M., Karlas, B., Rausch, J., Zhang, C. and Krause, A. (2021). Online Active Model Selection for Pre-trained Classifiers. - Liu, X., Xia, F., Stevens, R. L. and Chen, Y. (2022). Contextual Active Model Selection. arXiv:2207.06030. Basis: abstract. Online contextual selection of the model per instance with an active query component, with regret and query-complexity bounds. Closeness: low.

- Kay, J., Van Horn, G., Maji, S., Sheldon, D. and Beery, S. (2025). Consensus-Driven Active Model Selection. ICCV 2025. DOI 10.1109/iccv51701.2025.00437; arXiv:2507.23771. Basis: abstract. - Ashury-Tahan, S., Gera, A., Sznajder, B., Choshen, L., Ein-Dor, L. and Shnarch, E. (2024). Label-Efficient Model Selection for Text Generation. Proceedings of ACL 2024 (Long Papers). - Durmazkeser, Y., Okanovic, P., Kirsch, A., Hoefler, T. and Gurel, N. M. (2025). Large Language Model Selection with Limited Annotations. arXiv:2510.09418 (preprint). Closeness: low-medium.
- Turrin, A., Okanovic, P., Hoefler, T. and Gurel, N. M. (2026). Which LLM to pick? Online Active Model Selection for Large Language Models. arXiv:2610.01592 (preprint). Basis: abstract. Online version for streams. Closeness: low.
- Dorner, F. E. and Hardt, M. (2024). Don't Label Twice: Quantity Beats Quality when Comparing Binary Classifiers on a Budget. arXiv:2402.02249 (the arXiv comment says ICML 2024; proceedings page not checked). Basis: abstract. To identify the more accurate of two classifiers under noisy labels, single labels on more points beat repeated labels with majority vote. Closeness: low (budget allocation across label repetitions, not across instances).

## 4. Fairness auditing and fairness estimation with few labels

- Yan, T. and Zhang, C. (2022). Active Fairness Auditing. ICML 2022, PMLR 162:24929-24962. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/yan22c.html; arXiv:2206.08450. Basis: abstract. Query-efficient estimation of a model's demographic parity: an optimal deterministic algorithm, a randomised oracle-efficient algorithm, and query-complexity results. Closeness: low-medium. The "labels" are queries to the audited model; demographic parity does not involve ground truth, so this does not cover TPR/FPR. One model.
- Ajarra, A., Ghosh, B. and Basu, D. (2024). Active Fourier Auditor for Estimating Distributional Properties of ML Models. arXiv:2410.08111 (preprint). Basis: abstract. Query-based auditing of robustness, individual and group fairness through Fourier coefficients of the audited model. Closeness: low (model-query setting).
- Ajarra, A. and Basu, D. (2026). Auditing Fairness under Model Updates. arXiv:2601.05909; and Efficient Active Auditing of Multi-Group Fairness with Bias Probes. arXiv:2609.40034 (preprints). Basis: abstracts. PAC auditing of statistical parity under strategic model updates; active learned "bias probes" for multi-group fairness. Closeness: low (model-query setting, one model).
- Hartmann, D., Pohlmann, L., Hanslik, L., Giessing, N., Berendt, B. and Delobelle, P. (2026). Audit Me If You Can: Query-Efficient Active Fairness Auditing of Black-Box LLMs. arXiv:2601.03087 (the arXiv comment says ACL Findings 2026; not checked on the ACL Anthology). Basis: abstract. BAFA keeps a version space of surrogates and gives uncertainty intervals for fairness metrics such as Delta-AUC, with active query selection; compares against stratified sampling and is reported to need up to 40 times fewer queries. Closeness: medium (active estimation of a group-performance gap that involves ground truth; stratified sampling as a baseline). Differences: one model, version-space intervals rather than design-based ones, no between-model comparison.
- Chugg, B., Cortes-Gomez, S., Wilder, B. and Ramdas, A. (2023). Auditing Fairness by Betting. NeurIPS 36. DOI 10.52202/075280-0266; arXiv:2305.17570. Basis: abstract. Sequential, anytime-valid tests of fairness; data may be collected by a probabilistic, changing policy, including different policies per subpopulation. Closeness: medium (non-uniform, group-dependent sampling with valid inference). Differences: one model, sequential testing rather than fixed-budget design optimisation, no disagreement strata.
- Cherian, J. J. and Candes, E. J. (2023). Statistical Inference for Fairness Auditing. arXiv:2305.03712 (preprint). Basis: abstract. Bootstrap simultaneous bounds on performance disparities over many (even infinite) subgroups for one model. Closeness: low-medium (simultaneous inference across groups, fully labelled data).
- Das, N., Sharma, M., Nanavati, P., Shiragur, K. and Deshpande, A. (2025). Cost Efficient Fairness Audit Under Partial Feedback. arXiv:2510.03734 (NeurIPS 2025 workshop paper per arXiv comment). Basis: abstract. Auditing under selective labels (only positively classified individuals observed), with a cost model for buying extra labels; covers demographic parity and equal opportunity. Closeness: low-medium. One model.
- Ji, D., Smyth, P. and Steyvers, M. (2020). Can I Trust My Fairness Metric? Assessing Fairness with Unlabeled Data and Bayesian Inference. NeurIPS 2020; arXiv:2010.09851. https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2020/file/d83de59e10227072a9c034ce10029c39-Paper.pdf. Basis: full text searched. Hierarchical Bayesian calibration of scores per group to infer group TPR/FPR/accuracy differences for one model. A footnote notes that "stratified sampling by group could help ... but stratification might not always be possible", and the related-work section names stratified, importance and active sampling as complementary. Closeness: medium-high on target quantities. Differences: one model, labelled data taken as given (no design), Bayesian.
- Gao, J. and Gronsbell, J. (2025). Reliable fairness auditing with semi-supervised inference. arXiv:2505.12181 (preprint). Basis: abstract and searched full text. "Infairness": imputation of missing outcomes by regression on nonlinear bases, then augmentation for robustness, for group-specific TPR, FPR and calibration metrics. About 50% variance reduction against supervised estimation. Closeness: medium-high on target quantities and semi-supervised efficiency. Differences: one model, labelled set assumed random (no designed allocation), no disagreement.
- Gronsbell, J., Liu, M., Tian, L. and Cai, T. (2022). Efficient Evaluation of Prediction Rules in Semi-Supervised Settings under Stratified Sampling. JRSS-B 84(4):1353-1391. DOI 10.1111/rssb.12502. Basis: abstract. Semi-supervised evaluation (Brier score, misclassification rate) of one prediction rule when the labelled data are a stratified sample, with weighted imputation and augmentation. Closeness: medium (stratified labelling plus unlabeled data for performance evaluation). Differences: one model, strata given, not optimised; no fairness.
- Shanmugam, D., Sadhuka, S., Raghavan, M., Guttag, J., Berger, B. and Pierson, E. (2025). Evaluating multiple models using labeled and unlabeled data. NeurIPS 38. DOI 10.52202/085713-1029; arXiv:2501.11866. Basis: abstract and searched full text. SSME fits a semi-supervised mixture model of the joint distribution of labels and the scores of several classifiers, and estimates any metric for each classifier, including within demographic subgroups. Closeness: high in setting (several classifiers, plentiful unlabeled data, subgroup performance). Differences: model-based (mixture model), labelled data taken as given (no sampling design), no use of disagreement strata for allocation, no design-based intervals for between-model fairness contrasts.

## 5. Prediction-powered and active inference

Closeness: medium (the predictions-as-control-variate principle that our proxy-based allocation also uses).
- Angelopoulos, A. N., Duchi, J. C. and Zrnic, T. (2026). PPI++: Efficient prediction-powered inference. Annals of Applied Statistics 20:2235-2252. DOI 10.1214/26-aoas2215; arXiv:2311.01453. Basis: abstract. Power-tuned PPI that never does worse than the classical interval. Natural baseline.

- Li, P., Zrnic, T. and Candes, E. (2025). Robust Sampling for Active Statistical Inference. NeurIPS 38, pp. 76645-76674. DOI 10.52202/085713-2312; arXiv:2511.08991. Basis: abstract. - Fisch, A. et al. (2026). CollabEval: Statistically Efficient Collaborative Model Evaluation via Matrix Completion. arXiv:2607.05046 (preprint). Basis: abstract. ## 7. Assessment

### 7.1 What is already done

1. Disagreement-focused labelling for comparing models is established. Sawade et al. (2012) derive the power-optimal sampling distribution for the risk difference of two models, note that it favours disagreement, include a disagreement-only baseline, and extend to several models by a mixture. Hara et al. (2024) target the variance of loss differences, CODA (2025) uses consensus and disagreement among K models, Musgrove et al. (2023) adjudicate only discordant pairs, and Kuboyama (2026) shows that disagreement rows settle accuracy rankings. In biostatistics, comparing two screening tests by verifying only discordant or test-positive subjects is decades old (Schatzkin et al. 1987; Chock et al. 1997; Pepe and Alonzo 2001), and covers TPR and FPR. Idea (1) cannot be claimed as new and must be cited.
2. Proxy-driven stratification with optimal (Neyman-type) allocation for estimating a classifier's metric is established: Bennett and Carvalho (2010), Kumar and Raj (2018), Katariya et al. (2012), McNamee (2002) for sensitivity and specificity, StratPPI (2024), approximate Neyman allocation for LLM active testing (2026), and stratified importance sampling (2026). Active inference with proxy-based inclusion probabilities and CLT intervals is established (Zrnic and Candes 2024; Li et al. 2025; Ashouritaklimi et al. 2026).
3. Label-efficient estimation of group TPR/FPR gaps for one model with plentiful unlabeled data is established: Ji et al. (2020, Bayesian), Gao and Gronsbell (2025, semi-supervised), SSME (2025, several models, with subgroups), with design-aware semi-supervised evaluation under stratified sampling in Gronsbell et al. (2022). Ji et al. explicitly mention stratifying by group as a possible improvement but do not develop it.
4. Selecting among several models on accuracy and subgroup fairness with costly labels exists only in an online bandit form (Kher et al. 2026, preprint).

### 7.2 What remains open (as far as this sweep can tell)

- No paper found designs the labelling of a fixed pool for comparing K > 2 classifiers on group-specific TPR, FPR and the equalised-odds gap. That means: strata defined by protected group crossed with the K-model disagreement pattern, allocation optimised for a vector of between-model fairness contrasts, and design-based (HT or ratio) estimators with linearisation intervals for those contrasts.
- The diagnostic-test designs identify only ratios when concordant cells are not verified, and Sawade's design handles risk only. Our estimands, such as TPR_A(g) - TPR_B(g) = [P(A=1, Y=1, G=g) - P(B=1, Y=1, G=g)] / P(Y=1, G=g), have numerators that depend only on disagreement cells but denominators (group prevalence) that depend on all cells. How to split labels between disagreement cells (numerators) and agreement cells (denominators), and the resulting variance of the ratio contrast, appears not to have been worked out for classifier comparison. This is a concrete technical gap and a defensible contribution.
- Allocation for many contrasts at once (K(K-1)/2 pairs x groups x {TPR, FPR}), for example compromise or minimax Neyman allocation, has no counterpart in the ML evaluation papers found. Sawade et al. use only a heuristic mixture.
- Inference for the equalised-odds gap, a maximum of absolute differences and so non-smooth at ties, and for differences of such gaps between models, under a stratified unequal-probability design: not found.
- Simultaneous intervals across models and metrics under such a design: not found (Chatzi et al. do simultaneous rank-sets under PPI, not under designed sampling or fairness).

### 7.3 Defensible novelty statement

"We give a fixed-budget sampling design for comparing several trained classifiers on group-specific error, TPR, FPR and equalised-odds gaps. The pool is stratified by protected group and by the models' disagreement pattern, labels are allocated by a Neyman-type rule computed from model-predicted probabilities, and between-model contrasts are estimated with design-unbiased Horvitz-Thompson and ratio estimators and linearisation confidence intervals. Disagreement-focused sampling for comparing risks (Sawade et al., 2012; Hara et al., 2024), discordant-result designs for comparing two diagnostic tests (Pepe and Alonzo, 2001), proxy-based optimal stratification for estimating one classifier's metrics (Kumar and Raj, 2018; Fisch et al., 2024), and label-efficient fairness estimation for one model (Ji et al., 2020; Gao and Gronsbell, 2025) are known. To our knowledge, their combination into a design for between-model fairness contrasts, with the treatment of group-prevalence denominators and of several contrasts at once, has not been proposed."

Avoid claiming: that using disagreement is new; that Neyman allocation with model proxies is new; that this is the first label-efficient fairness estimation; or that it is the first method to compare several models with few labels.

### 7.4 Baselines a reviewer will expect

1. Simple random sampling of the same budget, plug-in estimates, with Wald or bootstrap intervals (the default).
2. Group-stratified sampling with proportional and with equal allocation (no disagreement strata), to isolate the gain from disagreement stratification.
3. Disagreement-only sampling (label only cells where some pair disagrees), with prevalence taken from a small random sample or assumed. This is the "active not-equal" / A2-IWAL style baseline of Sawade et al. and the discordant-pair approach of Musgrove et al.; it shows why agreement cells must also be sampled.
4. Sawade et al. (2012) active comparison (importance sampling, pairwise mixture for K models), adapted to TPR/FPR contrasts.
5. Active testing with LURE (Kossen et al. 2021), run per model or on the loss difference (Hara et al. 2024).
6. Prediction-powered estimators: PPI++ and StratPPI applied per group and per model with the models' probabilities as predictions, and active inference (Zrnic and Candes 2024) with robust sampling (Li et al. 2025).
7. Semi-supervised or model-based estimators with uniformly sampled labels: Ji et al. (2020) Bayesian calibration, Infairness (Gao and Gronsbell 2025), SSME (Shanmugam et al. 2025).
8. If model selection is claimed: Model Selector (Okanovic et al. 2025), CODA (Kay et al. 2025) and online active model selection (Karimi et al. 2021), compared on the probability of picking the fairest or the best accuracy-fairness model.
9. An oracle Neyman allocation (true cell variances) as an upper reference, and the full-label estimate as ground truth.

Metrics to report: RMSE of each contrast, coverage and width of the intervals (including in small groups), labels needed to reach a target width or to detect a given difference, and sensitivity to poor proxy calibration.
